      The trillion-dollar prospectus, annotated.

      Anthropic's IPO filing runs to 421 pages. It shows quarterly revenue up thirtyfold in five quarters, a first operating profit, an $80.7 billion loss that is almost all paper, and close to a trillion dollars promised for computing power. The share price is still a blank. Here is what the filing says, how to read it, and what its sharpest critics and defenders are arguing about.

      The five-minute version

      Seven things to know before you read anything else

        Growth S-1 pp. 157–160, 181–196

        From $376 million a quarter to $11.6 billion

        Anthropic's revenue grew 30-fold in five quarters. Almost all of the jump came in the first half of 2026.

        Before you look: how much revenue did Anthropic book in the three months to June 30, 2026?

        The S-1 shows revenue of $4.6 billion for all of 2025 and $16.3 billion for the first six months of 2026p.F-7. Second-quarter revenue alone was $11.6 billion, up 1,368% on a year earlierp.157. The company credits successive model and product launches, especially Claude Code and the Opus releases, which pulled spending up across existing customers.

        A second check. Trailing revenue per employee of "over $6.6 million" with about 5,300 staffp.10 implies roughly $35B of revenue in the year to September. Subtract the three known quarters and Q3 lands between about $16B and $18B.

        The filing leaves third-quarter revenue blank, but it gives the answer away. It says Q3 revenue was 86 times revenue in the fourth quarter of 2024p.183, which was $197 millionp.63. That makes Q3 about $16.9 billion, close to the $16.8 billion Anthropic reportedly gave investors on October 14. It also means quarter-on-quarter growth slowed from 144% in Q2 to about 46%. Still extraordinary, but slower, and the bears have noticed (see the debates).

        Who is paying

        Customers paying at least $100,000 a year (the S-1's stand-in for "enterprise") went from about 20 at the end of 2023 to more than 11,900 at the end of September 2026p.181. More than 1,900 pay over $1 million and about 250 pay over $10 million. About 80% of first-half revenue was usage-based (paying per token through the API) and 20% came from subscriptions such as Claude Pro, Max, Team and Enterprisep.F-26.

        Three caveats the bulls skip

        • Net revenue retention of "approximately 900%" is not a SaaS number. A customer counts if it paid at least $1 in the base quarter. Measured against a tiny base a year ago, the ratio explodes. Typical software companies report 110–130%p.2.
        • Some revenue came from customers who broke the rules. An estimated 5–7% of revenue in the first nine months of 2026 came from customers later found to be violating the usage policy or commercial terms. Enforcement stepped up in August and September and is expected to reduce that revenuep.145.
        • Half the money arrives through rivals' clouds. 48% of first-half revenue came through Amazon, Google and Microsoft marketplaces, up from 11% in 2023. Those same companies hold 58% of Anthropic's receivablesp.56.

        The growth is real and audited. But a fifth of it rests on five customers, half of it is collected by competitors, and part of it came from customers the company is now cutting off.

        Unit economics S-1 pp. 10, 157–160, 182

        Each dollar of compute now brings in about two dollars of revenue

        In early 2025 Anthropic spent $2.51 on computing for every dollar it earned. By mid-2026 that had flipped. The second quarter of 2026 was its first with an operating profit.

        The S-1 does not split costs into "cost of revenue" and operating expenses, so there is no gross margin to quote. Instead it reports one big line, compute and infrastructure, which covers both training new models and serving customersp.157. The chart below sets that line against revenue, quarter by quarter.

        Why the ratio jumped, and why it may not last

        Compute costs here behave like rent. Anthropic signs up for capacity 12 to 18 months ahead, and the expense starts when the capacity is delivered, whatever demand turns out to bep.158. In the first half of 2026, demand outran the capacity that showed up, so revenue per compute dollar soared.

        The bill is about to step up. One contract alone, with SpaceX's AI unit, runs at about $3.1 billion a month once fully delivered, which the company expects in December 2026p.171. That is more than half the entire second-quarter compute line, every month. The next few quarters will show whether revenue keeps pace with the capacity arriving.

        Why "adjusted" flatters. Stock-based pay was $1.26B in Q2 2026 alone and is growing fast. At the IPO, restricted stock units add a $2.9B catch-up charge and $23.7B more over about two yearsp.64.

        Because we typically procure compute 12 to 18 months in advance of availability, these expenses are generally fixed in the near term and increased as committed compute became available to us rather than in response to in-period demand.

        For the first time, Anthropic's revenue covers its compute bill twice over. That ratio will be tested as hundreds of billions of dollars of contracted capacity is delivered.

        The $80.7 billion loss S-1 pp. 63–64, 157–160, F-7

        Why the company's best half-year shows its biggest loss

        Anthropic lost $693 million running its business in the first half of 2026. It lost $80.7 billion on paper. The difference comes from a pile of IOUs that grew more valuable as the company did.

        Between 2023 and 2025, Amazon and Google lent Anthropic billions through convertible notes. These are loans that turn into shares at a price fixed in advance. As Anthropic's valuation climbed, the shares those notes would become were worth more. Accounting rules require the company to record that increase as an expense, called a change in fair value. That line was $72.4 billion in the first half of 2026p.F-7. Most of it ($59.6 billion) landed in the second quarter, when the Series H round reset the valuationp.159.

        A further $8.4 billion of "other expense" in that quarter came from the first-time valuation of new financing facilities from Google, Amazon and Broadcomp.160. No cash left the building for either item. Interest earned on a $131.7 billion cash pile added $1.1 billion going the other way.

        The perverse part. The better the company does before the IPO, the bigger this loss looks. It stops when the notes convert into stock at the IPO. Amazon's $6.2B of notes outstanding at June 30 convert into non-voting shares, capped at 12% ownership unless Amazon waives the capp.164.

        The expense was largest in the three months ended June 30, 2026, reflecting the increase in our enterprise valuation in connection with our Series H financing. We will continue to recognize changes in fair value until these instruments are converted into equity, repaid, or otherwise settled.

        What the loss does tell you

        The paper loss tells you little about the business. It does tell you who now owns the company. Each markup is value that moved from existing shareholders to the noteholders. The real cash question sits in the compute contracts, the next section.

        Read "net loss" here as "how much richer Amazon and Google's IOUs got." The operating line is the one that shows how the business is doing, and it turned positive in Q2 2026.

        The compute bill S-1 pp. 137, 169–172, F-35, F-58, F-65

        The filing leaves the total blank. We added it up.

        Anthropic has signed roughly $855 billion of minimum payments for chips, data centers and cloud capacity. It estimates another $145–170 billion on deals whose prices aren't final. That comes to about a trillion dollars, more than 40 times its revenue over the past year.

        As of and subsequent to September 30, 2026, we have entered into purchase commitments and lease agreements, including short-term commitments, as well as leases not yet commenced, with an aggregate committed spend of more than . These commitments include approximately expected to be incurred before December 31, 2030, and substantially all of the remainder is expected to be incurred between 2031 and 2045.

        The arithmetic, all from the financial statement notes. At June 30, 2026: $265.6 billion of fixed or minimum purchase commitmentsp.F-58, $321.4 billion of leases signed but not yet startedp.F-35, and $2.1 billion of leases already running. After June 30: about $245 billion of new long-term deals and $21 billion of short-term minimumsp.F-65. Total: about $855 billion. The company also estimates $125–150 billion for extra TPU capacity from 2028 to 2031 and more than $20 billion for AMD systems, neither of which it counts in its own tablep.170.

        Why the total is blank. The S-1 speaks as of September 30 and later, but its audited notes stop at June 30. The total will most likely be filled in by an amendment before pricing. Our figure uses only what the filing already discloses.

        Who it's owed to is spelled out deal by dealp.170. Some are firm minimums Anthropic must pay "regardless of actual usage", with cash true-ups for any shortfall. Others are "up to" amounts or can be cancelled.

        Can they pay?

        The S-1 does not give a year-by-year schedule, so we built a rough one. Each named deal is spread evenly over its stated term, and unnamed commitments are spread to 2040. Then set your own assumptions for revenue growth and for how much revenue each compute dollar earns (2.08 in Q2 2026). The tool shows the year the contracted bill outruns what revenue can carry, and how much of the cash pile the gap would eat.

        Anthropic has bet that demand keeps compounding. At today's efficiency, paying for 2027's contracted compute out of revenue alone would take roughly $260 billion of revenue, nearly five times our 2026 estimate. With the cash pile absorbing the gap, growth of about 150% in 2027 is enough. Much slower growth means new money or cancelled contracts.

        The money goes round S-1 pp. 56, 73, 163–172, F-16, F-39–F-42

        Its biggest investors are also its biggest suppliers

        Amazon, Google, Broadcom and AMD each put money into Anthropic, and each is owed far more for chips and cloud capacity. Several of those investments only unlock as the investor delivers its own hardware.

        Critics call this circular financing: a supplier funds its customer so the customer can buy from the supplier. Defenders call it ordinary vendor financing that lets a fast-growing buyer lock in scarce capacity. The S-1 gives enough detail to judge each relationship on its own terms.

        Four details worth knowing

        • Amazon's next $15 billion arrives in $5 billion steps "following the achievement of the relevant compute capacity milestones", meaning capacity Amazon delivers. Amazon can also settle its notes by netting them against money it owes Anthropic from marketplace salesp.165.
        • Google's $20 billion facility is sized to TPU capacity delivered and to revenue targets. Google also holds an option to buy up to $10 billion of stock alongside the IPO at the IPO pricep.168.
        • Broadcom leases Anthropic about $161 billion of TPU hardware and offers a $42 billion facility whose only purpose is to lend Anthropic the rent. It accrues 9% interest (13% if revenue targets are missed) and converts into stock at a 10% discount to the market pricep.166.
        • AMD sells Anthropic more than $20 billion of GPU systems and has agreed to buy up to $5 billion of its stock as delivery milestones are metp.163.

        Hiding in plain sight. Amazon and Google each own more than 5% of Anthropic. They hold non-voting Class N shares, and the related-party rules key off voting stock, so neither appears in the "Related Person Transactions" section. Their deals are in the MD&A insteadp.282.

        The circularity is real and disclosed. It ties Anthropic tightly to a few partners who are also its competitors, but most of the commitment runs one way: Anthropic owes its partners several times what they have put in.

        Who controls Anthropic S-1 pp. 107–118, 242–253, 288–306

        One share with 50.1% of the vote, and a trust that picks the board

        Public investors will own a slice of the profits but have almost no say. The filing calls the structure "novel" with "no direct public company precedent."

        There are five classes of common stockp.292. Class A is what you can buy: one vote per share. Class N is the same economically but carries no vote, and it's held mostly by strategic partners such as Amazon and Google. Class F is a single share held by an LLC of the seven co-founders. It always carries 50.1% of the stockholder vote and has no economic rights. Class T is a single share held by the Long-Term Benefit Trust, which elects four of the seven directors. Class E gives employees a vote on a few governance matters.

        The trust

        The trust has four trustees, with a fifth seat vacant: Neil "Buddy" Shah (chair, CEO of the Clinton Health Access Initiative), Richard Fontaine (CEO of the Center for a New American Security), former Federal Reserve chair Ben Bernanke, and Harvard physicist Christopher Stubbsp.245. Each is paid $400,000 a year and may not own Anthropic stock. Anthropic funds the trust and consolidates it on its own balance sheetp.246. The trust's directors are Reed Hastings, Novartis CEO Vas Narasimhan and former Microsoft CFO Christopher Liddell, plus one open seat. The founders' three seats are Dario Amodei, Daniela Amodei and Spark Capital's Yasmin Razavip.256.

        Small print, big deal. Anthropic must give the trust five days' notice before it can "transfer control of an AI model exceeding capabilities thresholds" set by the board each quarter. A transfer without that notice is voidp.285.

        You are buying an economic stake in a company run by its founders and overseen by a trust, with a legal duty to weigh "the long-term benefit of humanity" alongside your returns. That is the deal on offer, and it is disclosed plainly.

        Risk factors S-1 pp. 27–119

        88 risk factors, and several no prospectus has carried before

        Risk factors are the part of an S-1 where lawyers list everything that could go wrong. Most are boilerplate. Anthropic's include disclosures about its own models behaving badly, written in securities-law language.

          How this was made

          The S-1 and its exhibits were downloaded from SEC EDGAR (CIK 2130357, accession 0001628280-26-067168), along with the six confidential drafts released the same day. They were converted to text with printed page numbers kept, and every figure here was checked against that text. Page chips (p.157) open the filing on SEC.gov; in most browsers they jump to the quoted words.

          Derived numbers are labelled as ours and show their arithmetic. Outside claims name their source and date. Where sources disagree, we say so. The compute model and the valuation tool are illustrations built on stated assumptions, not forecasts.
