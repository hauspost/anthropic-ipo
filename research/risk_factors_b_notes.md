# Risk Factors (printed pp. 60–119): the 15 most newsworthy items

Source: Anthropic, PBC Form S-1 (filed 2026-10-26), Risk Factors, printed pages 60–119. Page cites are printed page numbers. All quotes are verbatim and were checked against the page text by script. IDs refer to `risk_factors_b.json` (68 risk factors, rf-b-001 to rf-b-068).

## 1. The Department of War dispute: a federal ban, a “supply-chain risk” designation, and split court results

Catalog: rf-b-010, rf-b-009

> “in response to our publicly stated position that our technology should not be used for mass domestic surveillance or fully autonomous weapons, the President of the United States stated that he had directed that all federal executive agencies cease using our technology” (p. 71)
>
> “the Secretary of War directed the Department of War to designate us as a “supply-chain risk to national security” and stated that “effective immediately, no contractor, supplier, or partner that does business with the United States military may conduct any commercial activity” with us.” (p. 71)
>
> “The court ruled that the Challenged Actions were retaliatory acts meant to punish us for speech protected under the First Amendment to the United States Constitution” (p. 72)
>
> “On September 25, 2026, in a 2-1 split decision, the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals denied our petition for review, ruling that the Department of War’s Section 4713 supply-chain risk designation was proper” (p. 72)

**Why it matters:** An IPO issuer disclosing that its own government designated it a national-security supply-chain risk is close to unprecedented. The legal picture is split. The N.D. Cal. granted summary judgment largely for Anthropic on Aug 27, 2026 (First Amendment retaliation, Fifth Amendment due process, and APA violations on the § 3252 track), but the separate FASCSA § 4713 designation stands after the D.C. Circuit's 2-1 panel ruling, and an en banc petition is pending. Direct government revenue is only about 1% of the total (p. 70), but the filing warns the dispute could deter contractors, partners and international customers.

## 2. The Pentagon threatened the Defense Production Act over Anthropic's weapons and surveillance limits, and those limits have cost revenue

Catalog: rf-b-009, rf-b-057

> “in early 2026, the U.S. Department of War suggested it could invoke the Defense Production Act to compel us to remove the limitations we have placed on the use of our models for autonomous weapons and mass surveillance” (p. 69)
>
> “our usage policy prohibits the use of our models, platform, and applications to develop or operate fully autonomous weapons or to conduct mass surveillance, including mass domestic surveillance, and we have declined to permit such uses based on our Company Purpose, notwithstanding the revenue those or similar uses could generate.” (p. 110)
>
> “Maintaining these limitations has had, and may continue to have, adverse commercial and financial consequences.” (p. 110)

**Why it matters:** This puts a price on a safety and ethics policy: Anthropic acknowledges that its red lines have already cost money and that the government floated forcing it to drop them. The DPA mention is buried in a bullet list inside the general regulatory risk factor.

## 3. Worldwide export restrictions forced Anthropic to switch off its flagship models for all customers

Catalog: rf-b-041, rf-b-009, rf-b-010

> “in June 2026, the U.S. Department of Commerce imposed worldwide export restrictions on our Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models. To ensure compliance, we disabled Fable 5 and Mythos 5 for all of our customers; access to our other models was not affected.” (p. 99)
>
> “Similar export controls could recur, could remain in place longer, or could extend to additional models or capabilities.” (p. 99)
>
> “we have in the past voluntarily disclosed, and may in the future voluntarily disclose, instances of noncompliance to the relevant authorities.” (p. 100)

**Why it matters:** The government restricted specific frontier models worldwide, not just exports to particular countries, and Anthropic turned them off for every customer until it updated its safeguards and the restrictions were lifted. This shows that product availability, and therefore revenue, can be cut off by regulators with little notice. The filing also admits past export or sanctions compliance lapses that it self-reported.

## 4. Compute commitments: a roughly $125B non-cancelable equipment lease and 20-year take-or-pay power/data-center deals

Catalog: rf-b-012, rf-b-011

> “under the 2026 Lease Arrangement, the rent payments over the five-year equipment lease are equal to an aggregate of approximately $125.2 billion excluding interest” (p. 74)
>
> “The 2026 Lease Arrangement is non-cancelable by the parties to such agreement during the applicable lease period except in the case of a default.” (p. 74)
>
> “certain of our dedicated power and data center infrastructure arrangements have initial terms of 20 years with annual escalators and include take-or-pay compute payment obligations and uncapped fuel cost pass-throughs that apply for the duration of the term regardless of actual utilization.” (pp. 75–76)
>
> “Certain of these arrangements also depend on the continued performance of credit support provided by a third party that is also a competitor” (p. 75)

**Why it matters:** The lease's roughly $125.2B in rent over five years (about $25B a year before interest) is a fixed obligation; for scale, revenue was $16.3B in H1 2026 and $4.6B in full-year 2025 (p. 62). Take-or-pay terms and uncapped fuel pass-throughs turn compute from a variable cost into a fixed one. Cash for the restricted account was deposited in April 2026 for Broadcom, and defaults could accelerate a substantial portion of the obligations. Reliance on a competitor's credit support is an unusual dependency. Together these change the cash-flow profile from paying as compute arrives to paying upfront, ahead of revenue.

## 5. An $80.7B net loss in H1 2026 that is mostly non-cash: convertible notes marked up as the valuation soared, plus a large RSU charge at the IPO

Catalog: rf-b-006

> “a net loss of $1.5 billion, $8.3 billion, and $42.0 billion for the years ended December 31, 2023, 2024, and 2025, respectively, and $80.7 billion for the six months ended June 30, 2026” (p. 63)
>
> “These net losses included change in fair value of convertible notes, derivative liabilities, and SAFEs of $1.0 billion, $5.4 billion, and $34.3 billion for the years ended December 31, 2023, 2024, and 2025, respectively, and $72.4 billion for the six months ended June 30, 2026” (pp. 63–64)
>
> “we would have recorded $2.9 billion of cumulative stock-based compensation expense related to the RSUs on that date, and an additional $23.7 billion of unrecognized stock-based compensation expense related to the RSUs, would be recognized over a weighted-average period of approximately 2.1 years” (p. 64)

**Why it matters:** Headlines will cite the $80.7B loss, but about $72.4B of it is a mark-to-market charge on the convertible notes, a side effect of the rising valuation rather than cash burn. Excluding that item, H1 2026 losses are roughly $8.3B (a derived figure; MD&A has the operating detail). Separately, double-trigger RSUs mean a large stock-compensation charge in the IPO quarter: $2.9B in catch-up plus $23.7B more over about 2.1 years, measured at June 30, 2026, before the additional pre-IPO RSU grant whose amount is still blank.

## 6. Hypergrowth, alongside an admission that revenue has been hard to forecast

Catalog: rf-b-004, rf-b-005

> “In 2023, 2024, and 2025, our revenue was $41 million, $386 million, and $4.6 billion, respectively, representing year-over-year growth rates of 841% and 1,088%, respectively.” (p. 62)
>
> “In the six months ended June 30, 2026, our revenue was $16.3 billion, representing a year-over-year growth rate of 1,300%.” (p. 62)
>
> “we have struggled to accurately forecast our revenue in the past and may struggle to do so in the future.” (p. 62)
>
> “the number of our employees increased from approximately 940 as of December 31, 2024 to approximately 5,300 as of September 30, 2026” (p. 63)

**Why it matters:** The growth figures are extraordinary for a company of this size, and the filing explicitly expects the growth rate to decline. Headcount grew about 5.6x in 21 months. Q3 2026 revenue is still blank in this draft and should appear in an amendment.

## 7. Some revenue comes from customers violating the usage policy, and a crackdown is expected to reduce it

Catalog: rf-b-007, rf-b-008

> “Our revenue in any period has also included, and we expect will continue to include, amounts from customers that we subsequently determine are using our models, platform, and applications in violation of our usage policy or commercial terms” (p. 65)
>
> “Customers may evade our controls, and customers whose access is restricted or terminated may re-register under new accounts.” (p. 65)
>
> “the enhancement of such efforts in August and September 2026, and the timing of any resulting restrictions or terminations, are expected to reduce the percentage of revenue from customers using our models, platform, and applications in violation of our usage policy or commercial terms” (p. 65)

**Why it matters:** It is rare for an issuer to say that part of its reported revenue, and of Run-Rate Revenue (p. 67), comes from customers it later restricts or terminates for policy violations. The filing calls this common in the industry. This bears on revenue quality and on near-term growth after the August-September 2026 enforcement step-up. The filing does not quantify the amount, which is a natural question for analysts.

## 8. Founder control: one Class F share with a fixed 50.1% of the vote, which cannot be diluted

Catalog: rf-b-052, rf-b-053, rf-b-065

> “Our Class F common stock will be entitled to an aggregate number of votes that equals 50.1% of the total voting power of our outstanding shares of capital stock” (p. 108)
>
> “the Founder LLC will have the ability to determine the outcome of all matters submitted to a vote of our stockholders generally for the foreseeable future” (p. 108)
>
> “holders of our Class A common stock generally will not be able to affect the outcome of any matter submitted to a vote of our stockholders or the election of our directors, regardless of how they vote” (p. 108)
>
> “if we were to issue 1,000,000 additional shares of Class A common stock, our Class F common stock would automatically be entitled to an incremental number of votes sufficient to cause the aggregate voting power of our Class F common stock to equal 50.1%” (p. 117)

**Why it matters:** Most dual-class companies give founders shares with extra votes per share, so founder control erodes as the share count grows or founders sell. Here control is a fixed percentage that grows automatically with new issuance (about 1,004,008 extra votes per 1,000,000 new shares). It ends at the Class F Sunset Time: the completion of a Class F Transition Period that begins once there have been fewer than three Continuing Founders for 90 consecutive days, or earlier if the share is redeemed (with a founder supermajority) or the Founder LLC dissolves (pp. 108–109). If the founders can't agree, the Class F share votes in proportion to the Class A votes cast (p. 108).

## 9. The Long-Term Benefit Trust elects a board majority through a non-economic Class T share, making Anthropic a “controlled company”

Catalog: rf-b-052, rf-b-053, rf-b-055

> “We have a novel capital and corporate governance structure that has no direct public company precedent.” (p. 107)
>
> “Through a special class of common stock, Class T common stock, the LTBT generally has the authority to elect and remove the Class T directors, who together will constitute a majority of the authorized members of our board of directors.” (p. 107)
>
> “The LTBT is intended to operate as an independent body of five financially disinterested Trustees, and our Class T common stock has no economic rights.” (p. 107)
>
> “The Class T common stock may also be transferred without restriction upon the receipt of the same approvals required to amend, terminate, or redeem it.” (p. 108)
>
> “we do not expect to rely on this exemption except with respect to our NGC, which will not consist entirely of independent directors.” (p. 109)

**Why it matters:** Board control is split: five trustees, whom LTBT policy bars from directly buying or holding Anthropic securities (p. 108), pick the board majority, and the founders control everything else. The structure also adds Class E stock for employees (excluding co-founders), which votes only on limited matters and has no economic rights (p. 108). Controlled-company status rests on the trust rather than an economic owner, which is unusual. Anthropic says it will use the exemption only for the nominating and governance committee. The Class T share's transferability needs careful framing: it requires the same approvals as amending or terminating the trust's rights.

## 10. PBC duties: directors are obligated to weigh the mission, and conflicts need not favor shareholders

Catalog: rf-b-057, rf-b-018, rf-b-063

> “our directors are not merely permitted, but obligated, to consider our Company Purpose and the interests of other stakeholders.” (p. 111)
>
> “we cannot guarantee that such a conflict would be resolved in favor of our stockholders’ monetary interests” (p. 111)
>
> “we have chosen not to develop certain commercially attractive offerings, such as image and video generation models, in order to direct our compute toward our research and safety priorities.” (p. 110)
>
> “Delaware law could make it easier for our board of directors to reject a hostile bid, even where the acceptance of such a bid would provide the greatest short-term financial yield to investors.” (p. 111)
>
> “These initiatives, which include the Initial Public Benefit Issuance and the future Public Benefit Issuances, have been, and may continue to be, substantial in scale, and we expect to significantly grow these efforts over time.” (p. 111)

**Why it matters:** This tells investors plainly that profit maximization is not the governing objective. The concrete examples are product choices already made (no image or video generation, Mythos gated via Project Glasswing) and equity Public Benefit Issuances that dilute holders and may go to nonprofits (p. 116). Only holders of at least 2% of shares or $2M worth can sue to enforce the balancing duty (DGCL § 367, p. 111).

## 11. The co-founders' 80% philanthropy pledge is non-binding, and their voting control stays the same as they give shares away

Catalog: rf-b-066

> “our co-founders have each pledged to dedicate 80% of their personal equity in Anthropic to philanthropic causes. These commitments are voluntary, revocable expressions of each co-founder’s philanthropic intent and do not constitute legally binding obligations.” (p. 118)
>
> “as our co-founders make donations in furtherance of this pledge, their personal economic interests in us will decrease while their voting control through the Founder LLC will remain unchanged” (p. 118)

**Why it matters:** This creates a potential source of future share supply (donations followed by sales by recipients) and explicitly widens the gap between the founders' economic stake and their control. The filing says it may reduce alignment with other stockholders. Some recipient vehicles have signed irrevocable proxy agreements with Anthropic (p. 118).

## 12. Lock-ups are tiered by holder and tied to earnings releases

Catalog: rf-b-066, rf-b-063

> “in the case of our co-founders and certain of our stockholders holding more than 0.5% of our fully diluted shares prior to this offering, on the earlier of (A) 6:00 AM Eastern Time on the second full trading day immediately following the public release of our quarterly financial results for the fiscal quarter ending September 30, 2027” (p. 117)
>
> “immediately after the close of the trading day on the 365th day after the date of this prospectus” (p. 117)
>
> “for the fiscal quarter ending March 31, 2027 and (B) immediately after the close of the trading day on the 180th day after the date of this prospectus” (pp. 117–118)
>
> “registration rights agreements entered into with counterparties to certain of our NPAs provide such counterparties with rights to cause us to register for resale after this offering” (p. 116)

**Why it matters:** There are two lock-up tiers. Founders and holders above 0.5% are locked up until the earlier of about 12 months or two trading days after Q3 2027 results; everyone else until the earlier of 180 days or two trading days after Q1 2027 results. There are also specified early-expiration provisions, and Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley can grant waivers. Some shares become sellable from the first trading day (p. 118). Noteholders' registration rights and the IRA's demand rights (IRA dated May 28, 2026) are further sources of overhang.

## 13. Copyright: a $1.5B Bartz settlement, music-publisher suits and uncapped indemnities

Catalog: rf-b-033, rf-b-027, rf-b-031, rf-b-039

> “in September 2025, we reached an agreement to settle a copyright infringement class action, captioned Bartz, et al. v. Anthropic PBC (“Bartz”), for $1.5 billion, which was approved by the district court in July 2026” (p. 92)
>
> “certain of our agreements require us to defend and indemnify the counterparty, without a cap on our liability, against third-party claims alleging that our models, or outputs generated by our models, infringe third-party intellectual property rights.” (p. 93)
>
> “Moreover, as in Bartz, insurance may not fully cover such claims” (p. 98)
>
> “could require us to retrain affected models from scratch at substantial cost.” (p. 87)

**Why it matters:** The Bartz settlement is a large, concrete cost, and it was approved in July 2026. Pending cases include Concord, BMG, Round Hill, Sony Music Publishing and Reddit (p. 92). Uncapped indemnities for customers and evaluators turn IP risk into open-ended contingent liability. The fair-use risk factor warns that a loss by another AI company could set precedent that binds Anthropic too (p. 86).

## 14. Antitrust: a class action alleging frontier labs agreed to slow AI development

Catalog: rf-b-039

> “In September 2026, a putative class action was filed against us and other developers of frontier AI models in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California, alleging that we and the other defendants agreed to restrain competition by slowing the pace at which we develop and improve our respective AI models” (p. 97)
>
> “have suggested that such collaboration may warrant a narrow exemption or safe harbor from antitrust laws.” (p. 97)
>
> “These proposals have drawn public opposition from political leaders, some of whom have characterized such efforts as anticompetitive regardless of the purpose of such exemption or safe harbor.” (p. 97)

**Why it matters:** This is where the safety mission meets antitrust law: coordinating on the pace of capability development can look like collusion. The plaintiffs are paid consumer subscribers seeking treble damages. The filing says the risk may push Anthropic to scale back its safety advocacy and industry collaboration.

## 15. Security: nation-state targeting of Mythos, admitted defensive gaps, and insiders who can be legally compelled

Catalog: rf-b-030, rf-b-002

> “in light of its advanced cyber vulnerability discovery capabilities, state-sponsored actors may conduct cyberattacks specifically targeting Mythos.” (p. 88)
>
> “we have operated in the past, and may in the future operate, for periods of time with known limitations or gaps in our defenses” (p. 88)
>
> “We have been and expect that we will continue to be targeted by individuals seeking employment under false identities” (p. 88)
>
> “such personnel, vendors, or operators may be legally compelled to cooperate with government authorities, including intelligence and security agencies, in ways contrary to our interests, such as by providing access to our systems, data, or model weights.” (p. 89)

**Why it matters:** This is an unusually candid description of model-weight theft risk, which the filing says could raise national-security concerns and could enable misuse outside Anthropic's safeguards. It also discloses physical threats to employees (p. 61) and agent-specific attack surfaces such as prompt injection and hijacked agent sessions (p. 89).
